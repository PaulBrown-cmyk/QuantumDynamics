\begin{abstract}
QuantumDynamics is a family of one-, two-, and three-coordinate, two-diabatic-state
wavepacket and reduced-density-matrix tools for hydrogen-atom transfer. It combines
split-operator propagation, optional Markovian or exponentially correlated
Langevin centroid damping, optional stochastic modulation of either well,
MPI-distributed trajectories, OpenMP/FFTW acceleration, and physical-unit
input/output. It also provides quantum-detailed-balance density dynamics,
strong-coupling non-Markovian HEOM, an exact finite spin-bath model,
MPI-IO single-file output, exact restart,
convergence campaigns, and controlled mechanism attribution. This note records
equations, controls, tested behavior, analysis capability, and method boundaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope and current status}

Current code supports reproducible pure-state trajectories, ensemble kinetics,
two- and three-coordinate wavepacket models, and finite-grid reduced density matrices under
weak-coupling Davies or strong-coupling HEOM dynamics.
Input validation, unit conversion, FFT ordering, random-stream portability,
restart identity, serial and parallel HDF5, state-selective damping, and output
safety have regression coverage. It remains a parameterized nuclear-dynamics
model, not a first-principles electronic-structure engine.

The available bath descriptions form a hierarchy rather than interchangeable
implementations. Closed split-operator propagation isolates coherent wavepacket
motion. Centroid Langevin dynamics adds a classical thermalization model to pure
states. Davies dynamics gives a weak-coupling quantum Markov semigroup with exact
detailed balance. HEOM resolves finite Gaussian bath memory nonperturbatively, while
the finite spin bath supplies an exact non-Gaussian reference with recurrences. A
scientific calculation should select the least elaborate level that contains the
relevant time scales and then test its regime assumptions; agreement between two
levels is stronger evidence than numerical convergence inside only one model.

\section{Dynamical model}

\subsection{Two-state Hamiltonian}

Wavefunction is
\begin{equation}
  \Psi(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_1(x,t)\\\psi_2(x,t)\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
with Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
  \hat H = -\frac{1}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \mathbb{1}_{2\times2}
  + \begin{pmatrix}V_{11}(x,t)&V_{12}(x)\\V_{12}(x)&V_{22}(x,t)\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Base diabatic wells are
\begin{align}
  V_{11}(x) &= \tfrac12 k_1(x-x_1)^2+c_{4,1}(x-x_1)^4+\Delta_1,\\
  V_{22}(x) &= \tfrac12 k_2(x-x_2)^2+c_{4,2}(x-x_2)^4+\Delta_2.
\end{align}
Quartic terms are enabled by anharmonic model selections. Gaussian coupling is
\begin{equation}
  V_{12}(x)=V_c\exp\left[-\frac{(x-x_c)^2}{2\sigma_c^2}\right],
\end{equation}
with an exponential alternative. Lower and upper adiabatic surfaces are the
eigenvalues of the local $2\times2$ diabatic potential matrix,
\begin{equation}
 V_{\pm}(x)=\frac{V_{11}+V_{22}}{2}\pm
 \sqrt{\left(\frac{V_{11}-V_{22}}{2}\right)^2+|V_{12}|^2}.
\end{equation}
At a diabatic crossing $V_{11}=V_{22}$, nonzero coupling opens an adiabatic gap
$2|V_{12}|$. For real coupling, the local mixing angle may be chosen through
$\tan 2\theta=2V_{12}/(V_{11}-V_{22})$. This makes clear why ``reactant'' and
``product'' populations are representation-dependent observables: the code reports
diabatic populations
\begin{equation}
 P_i(t)=\int |\psi_i(x,t)|^2\,dx,\qquad P_1+P_2=\lVert\Psi\rVert^2,
\end{equation}
whereas adiabatic populations require the coordinate-dependent rotation by
$\theta(x)$. Diabatic representation avoids explicit derivative couplings, but a
first-principles diabatic matrix must retain a consistent electronic phase and state
character across geometry.

\subsection{Split propagation}

One Hamiltonian step uses symmetric potential splitting,
\begin{equation}
  e^{-i\hat H\Delta t}\approx e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/2}
  e^{-i\hat T\Delta t}e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/2}.
\end{equation}
Local potential exponentials use an analytic Hermitian $2\times2$ form.
Kinetic propagation uses forward and inverse FFTW transforms. Dedicated plans
are created for both diabatic components. Even and odd grid sizes have tested
FFT frequency ordering.

This symmetric, time-reversible Strang factorization has local error
$O(\Delta t^3)$ and accumulated fixed-time error $O(\Delta t^2)$ when the
commutators of $\hat T$ and $\hat V$ are sufficiently regular. Each substep is
unitary, so the discrete norm is conserved to roundoff when damping and absorption
are disabled. In Fourier space,
\begin{equation}
 \hat T\longrightarrow \frac{k^2}{2m},\qquad
 k_n=\frac{2\pi n}{L},
\end{equation}
and kinetic propagation is diagonal. FFT representation also imposes a periodic
box and a Nyquist cutoff. Grid-spacing convergence therefore tests unresolved high
momenta, while box-size and absorber tests diagnose periodic-image return. The
method follows the split spectral construction of Feit, Fleck, and Steiger
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/0021-9991(82)90091-2}{JCP 47, 412 (1982)}) and the
symmetric composition of Strang
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1137/0705041}{SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 5, 506 (1968)}).

\subsection{Momentum Langevin bath}

Markovian damping uses an exact finite-step Ornstein--Uhlenbeck centroid update
consistent with classical fluctuation--dissipation statistics. Colored damping
uses an exponential memory kernel,
\begin{equation}
  \gamma(t)=\frac{\gamma}{\tau_c}e^{-t/\tau_c},\qquad
  \tau_c=\frac{2}{\mathrm{FWHM}},
\end{equation}
with auxiliary memory and stationary random-force variables. The controls
\code{damp_reactant} and \code{damp_product} select the diabatic component that
receives the momentum translation. Enabling both recovers shared-centroid
damping.

For white damping, the free centroid momentum update is the exact
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck transition
\begin{equation}
 \bar p_{n+1}=e^{-\gamma\Delta t}\bar p_n+
 \sqrt{\frac{m}{\beta}\left(1-e^{-2\gamma\Delta t}\right)}\,\xi_n,
 \qquad \xi_n\sim\mathcal N(0,1),
\end{equation}
which preserves the Maxwell variance $\langle\bar p^2\rangle=m/\beta$ for any
time step. Colored damping represents
\begin{equation}
 \dot{\bar p}(t)=F(t)-\int_0^t\gamma(t-s)\bar p(s)\,ds+R(t),\qquad
 \langle R(t)R(t')\rangle=\frac{m}{\beta}\gamma(|t-t'|).
\end{equation}
The exponential kernel admits a Markovian embedding with one memory coordinate and
one OU random force, avoiding storage of the full momentum history. This is the
classical generalized Langevin FDT described by Kubo
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1088/0034-4885/29/1/306}{Rep. Prog. Phys. 29, 255
(1966)}).

This thermostat is a classical-FDT pure-state construction. The QGLE label does
not imply a full quantum-frequency-dependent fluctuation--dissipation kernel.
Because the kick acts on a wavepacket centroid, it constrains selected first moments
and their thermal variance; it does not by itself generate a completely positive
reduced-density evolution or the frequency-dependent zero-point fluctuations of a
quantum bath. State-selective damping is therefore a controlled phenomenological
model whose sensitivity must be checked by comparing reactant-only, product-only,
both-state, and bath-free calculations.

\subsection{Quantum-FDT density-matrix path}

\code{quantum_fdt_density.py} builds the full finite-grid system Hamiltonian,
diagonalizes it, and propagates the reduced density matrix with a completely
positive secular Davies generator. Quantum and classical FDT differ most clearly
in frequency space. Up to spectral-density convention, equilibrium quantum noise
has
\begin{equation}
 S_{\rm sym}(\omega)\propto J(|\omega|)
 \coth\left(\frac{\beta|\omega|}{2}\right),\qquad
 S(-\omega)=e^{-\beta\omega}S(\omega).
\end{equation}
The first relation includes zero-point fluctuations; the second is the KMS
absorption/emission asymmetry. Classical FDT is recovered only when
$\beta|\omega|\ll1$, where $\coth(\beta|\omega|/2)\simeq2/(\beta|\omega|)$.
Thus temperature alone does not determine whether classical noise is adequate:
all dynamically important Bohr frequencies must also satisfy the high-temperature
condition. See the quantum FDT development of Kubo
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/0031-8914(68)90101-8}{Physica 38, 349 (1968)}).

If $H_S|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$, the bath
coupling operator is resolved into Bohr-frequency components of $Q$. For each
positive frequency $\omega=E_n-E_m$, Drude--Ohmic emission and absorption rates
are
\begin{align}
 \Gamma_{n\rightarrow m} &= gJ(\omega)[n_B(\omega)+1]|Q_{mn}|^2,\\
 \Gamma_{m\rightarrow n} &= gJ(\omega)n_B(\omega)|Q_{mn}|^2,
\end{align}
where $n_B(\omega)=(e^{\beta\omega}-1)^{-1}$ and the implemented dimensionless
spectral shape is
\begin{equation}
 J(\omega)=\frac{\omega/\omega_c}{1+(\omega/\omega_c)^2}.
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\Gamma_{m\rightarrow n}}{\Gamma_{n\rightarrow m}}
=e^{-\beta\omega}.
\end{equation}
Energy populations obey the Pauli equation
\begin{equation}
 \dot p_n=\sum_{m\ne n}\left(\Gamma_{m\rightarrow n}p_m
 -\Gamma_{n\rightarrow m}p_n\right),
\end{equation}
while nondegenerate coherences rotate at their Bohr frequencies and decay at one
half the sum of outgoing population rates plus zero-frequency pure dephasing.
This KMS relation enforces quantum detailed balance at every resolved transition
and makes the Gibbs density matrix stationary. Stored output includes the full
complex density matrix, physical coordinate densities, populations, energies,
and a KMS residual. This is the controlled weak-coupling, Markovian quantum-FDT
limit. Strong-coupling non-Markovian quantum baths require HEOM or an equivalent
influence-functional method and are outside this solver's declared regime. They
are handled by the separate HEOM path below.

Davies construction is controlled by weak system--bath coupling, bath correlation
times short relative to relaxation, and secular separation of distinct Bohr
frequencies. Secularization guarantees complete positivity but suppresses coupling
between populations and coherences at nearly degenerate transitions. Consequently,
trace preservation, positivity, and KMS stationarity verify internal consistency;
they do not prove that weak-coupling or secular assumptions fit a chosen physical
system. This limit follows Davies' Markovian weak-coupling construction
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01608389}{Commun. Math. Phys. 39, 91 (1974)}).

\subsection{Strong-coupling non-Markovian HEOM}

\code{heom_nonmarkovian.py} treats a harmonic Gaussian environment with linear
system coupling operator $Q$ and Drude--Lorentz spectral density
\begin{equation}
 J(\omega)=\frac{2\lambda\gamma\omega}{\omega^2+\gamma^2},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is reorganization energy and $\gamma^{-1}$ is bath memory time.
The finite-temperature bath correlation is decomposed as
\begin{equation}
 C(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_k e^{-\nu_k t}+2\Delta_K\delta(t),
\end{equation}
with one Drude pole, $K$ Matsubara poles, and residual white-tail correction
$\Delta_K$. In atomic units, the retained expansion uses
\begin{align}
 \nu_0&=\gamma,&
 c_0&=\lambda\gamma\left[\cot\left(\frac{\beta\gamma}{2}\right)-i\right],\\
 \nu_k&=\frac{2\pi k}{\beta},&
 c_k&=\frac{4\lambda\gamma\nu_k}{\beta(\nu_k^2-\gamma^2)},\quad k\ge 1,
\end{align}
and chooses the residual term so that
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{\operatorname{Re}c_k}{\nu_k}+\Delta_K
 =\frac{2\lambda}{\beta\gamma}.
\end{equation}
This sum rule preserves the exact integrated real bath correlation after truncating
the Matsubara series. Scaled auxiliary density operators
$\tilde\rho_{\boldsymbol n}$ obey
\begin{align}
 \dot{\tilde\rho}_{\boldsymbol n}={}&-
 \left(i\mathcal L+\sum_k n_k\nu_k+\Delta_K Q^\times Q^\times\right)
 \tilde\rho_{\boldsymbol n} \nonumber\\
 &-i\sum_k\sqrt{(n_k+1)|c_k|}\,Q^\times
 \tilde\rho_{\boldsymbol n+\boldsymbol e_k} \nonumber\\
 &-i\sum_k\sqrt{\frac{n_k}{|c_k|}}
 \left(c_k Q\tilde\rho_{\boldsymbol n-\boldsymbol e_k}
 -c_k^*\tilde\rho_{\boldsymbol n-\boldsymbol e_k}Q\right).
\end{align}
The root ADO is the physical reduced density matrix; higher ADOs retain bath
memory and system--bath correlations. An occupation vector
$\boldsymbol n=(n_0,\ldots,n_K)$ records how often each exponential bath mode enters
the hierarchy. Truncating at $\sum_k n_k\le L$ gives
\begin{equation}
 N_{\rm ADO}=\binom{L+K+1}{K+1},
\end{equation}
before multiplying by the square of the retained system dimension. Scaling by
powers of $|c_k|$ makes neighboring hierarchy tiers numerically comparable and
improves conditioning. No Born, Markov, or secular approximation is used within
the declared harmonic-bath model. Hierarchy depth, Matsubara count, and retained
system states are explicit convergence axes. The declared regime assumes a
harmonic Gaussian bath, linear
coupling, and Drude spectral density. This follows
the nonperturbative reduced-hierarchy construction of Ishizaki and Tanimura
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1143/JPSJ.74.3131}{JPSJ 74, 3131 (2005)}) and scaled
hierarchy stabilization of Shi \emph{et al.}
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3077918}{JCP 130, 084105 (2009)}).

Factorized, correlated-equilibrium, and correlated-projected preparations are
available. Correlated equilibrium is obtained by solving the sparse stationary
truncated HEOM with a unit-trace constraint; a system preparation operator is then
applied to every ADO for correlated nonequilibrium initialization. At converged
hierarchy and bath expansion, the stationary real-time hierarchy corresponds to the
thermal hierarchy generated along imaginary time, as established by Tanimura
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4890441}{JCP 141, 044114 (2014)}).

Factorized initialization sets all non-root ADOs to zero and therefore omits initial
system--bath correlations. Stationary initialization instead solves
$\mathcal L_{\rm HEOM}\boldsymbol\rho_{\rm eq}=0$ together with
$\operatorname{Tr}\rho_{\boldsymbol 0}=1$. Applying a system operator $A$ to every
tier,
\begin{equation}
 \rho_{\boldsymbol n}(0)=
 \frac{A\rho^{\rm eq}_{\boldsymbol n}A^\dagger}
 {\operatorname{Tr}(A\rho^{\rm eq}_{\boldsymbol 0}A^\dagger)},
\end{equation}
creates a correlated nonequilibrium preparation without discarding bath memory.
Stationary residual alone is insufficient: root positivity, trace, Hermiticity, ADO
activity, and stability against $L$, $K$, and system-basis enlargement remain
required.

A 100~fs strong-coupling validation used $\lambda=500$~cm$^{-1}$,
$\gamma^{-1}=25$~fs, ten retained system states, two Matsubara terms, and
hierarchy depth four (35 ADOs). The reorganization/median-gap ratio was 4.47,
maximum ADO norm was 0.966, trace and Hermiticity errors were below
$5\times10^{-16}$, and the minimum root-density eigenvalue was
$-1.3\times10^{-16}$. Depth, bath-pole, and system-basis variants passed a
0.02 population tolerance; the largest population change was
$1.70\times10^{-4}$.

A separate 10~K validation used eight Matsubara terms, hierarchy depth three,
eight retained system states, and 220 ADOs. Depth four, a ninth Matsubara pole,
and two extra system states all passed the same tolerance; the largest population
change was $3.05\times10^{-4}$. Low-temperature calculations therefore remain
supported, but require additional bath poles and convergence checks.

\subsection{Exact non-Gaussian spin bath}

\code{spin_bath_non_gaussian.py} exactly diagonalizes a two-state transfer system
coupled to finite spin-$1/2$ modes,
\begin{equation}
 H=H_S+\sum_j\frac{\omega_j}{2}\sigma_j^z+
 Q\sum_j g_j\sigma_j^x.
\end{equation}
Thermal product-bath initialization, exact unitary evolution, and exact partial trace
yield the reduced density matrix without Gaussian, Born, Markov, or secular
assumptions. Specifically,
\begin{equation}
 \rho(0)=\rho_S(0)\otimes\frac{e^{-\beta H_B}}{Z_B},\qquad
 \rho_S(t)=\operatorname{Tr}_B\!\left[e^{-iHt}\rho(0)e^{iHt}\right].
\end{equation}
Unlike a harmonic bath, spin fluctuations are bounded and possess nonzero higher
cumulants, so two-point Gaussian correlation data do not determine the full bath
influence. Exact diagonalization retains those statistics and every finite-size
recurrence.

Output includes trace distance
\begin{equation}
 D(\rho_1,\rho_2)=\frac12\lVert\rho_1-\rho_2\rVert_1
\end{equation}
for reactant- and product-initialized states. Any interval with increasing $D$
witnesses recovery of state distinguishability and hence information backflow in
the Breuer--Laine--Piilo sense
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.103.210401}{PRL 103, 210401 (2009)}).
The reported sum of positive discrete increments is a witness for this chosen state
pair, not the optimization over all initial pairs required by the formal BLP measure.
This finite non-Gaussian reference model follows exact spin-star treatments such as Semin,
Sinayskiy, and Petruccione
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.89.012107}{PRA 89, 012107 (2014)}).
Hilbert-space cost is exponential, so the implementation enforces at most ten bath
spins; it does not represent an arbitrary macroscopic non-Gaussian environment.
Because Hilbert dimension is $2^{N_B+1}$ and dense diagonalization scales cubically
in that dimension, this path is a benchmark for small structured baths rather than a
continuum solver. Apparent relaxation must also be distinguished from dephasing
followed by finite-size revival.

\subsection{Stochastic potential bath}

Either diabatic well can receive coordinate-shift, curvature, or enthalpy
modulation. Noise can be white or Ornstein--Uhlenbeck colored and can be shared
or independent between wells. Colored and white processes begin with their
stationary Gaussian marginal distribution. Mode-dependent input amplitudes use
length, force-constant, or energy units as appropriate.

For colored modulation, each active surface variable follows the exact discrete OU
map
\begin{equation}
 R_{n+1}=aR_n+\sigma\sqrt{1-a^2}\,\xi_n,\qquad
 a=e^{-\Delta t/\tau_c},qquad \tau_c=2/\mathrm{FWHM},
\end{equation}
giving $\langle R_n^2\rangle=\sigma^2$ and
$\langle R(t)R(0)\rangle=\sigma^2e^{-|t|/\tau_c}$. Shared noise models common-mode
environmental motion; independent noise models separate local environments. Common-
mode enthalpy shifts largely cancel from the instantaneous diabatic energy gap,
whereas anticorrelated or independent shifts broaden it. Coordinate-shift and
curvature modes also change forces, so equal numerical $\sigma$ values across modes
do not represent equal physical perturbations.

\subsection{Absorbing boundaries}

Optional smooth edge masks suppress periodic FFT wraparound. Within an edge
layer of width $w$, each amplitude is multiplied after a full time step by
\begin{equation}
  M(x)=\exp\left[-\Gamma_{\rm abs}\Delta t
  \left(\frac{d_{\rm edge}(x)}{w}\right)^p\right].
\end{equation}
Absorbed norm represents outgoing flux. Norm-conservation tests apply only when
edge absorption is disabled.

The mask is equivalent to a finite-step non-Hermitian edge potential. It prevents
wraparound only when outgoing density enters the layer smoothly and is attenuated
before reaching the periodic boundary. Too narrow or too strong a layer reflects
high-wavenumber components; too weak a layer leaks them through. Production results
must therefore be stable under independent variation of layer width, rate, power,
and box size. Population lost to the mask cannot be interpreted as reaction yield
unless the absorbing region has a physically defined channel meaning.

\subsection{Two-coordinate propagation}

\code{multidimensional_transfer.py} propagates
$\Psi(x,y,t)=(\psi_1,\psi_2)^T$ with a two-dimensional FFT split operator.
Coordinate $x$ is the H-transfer coordinate and $y$ is a promoting or bath mode
with independent mass. Shifted harmonic valleys contain reaction-path coupling,
and the diabatic coupling is Gaussian in both coordinates. The solver writes
physical-unit two-dimensional densities and potential surfaces to HDF5.

For separable Cartesian-like coordinates, the kinetic operator is
\begin{equation}
 \hat T=-\frac{1}{2m_x}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}
        -\frac{1}{2m_y}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2},
\end{equation}
so the multidimensional FFT keeps the kinetic exponential diagonal. A promoting
mode can alter valley geometry, crossing accessibility, and effective coupling even
when it is not itself a dissipative bath. Thus a change relative to one-dimensional
dynamics may reflect coherent mode coupling rather than decoherence.

\subsection{Three-coordinate propagation and external PES data}

\code{multidimensional_transfer_3d.py} propagates
$\Psi(x,y,z,t)$ using a three-dimensional FFT split operator. Coordinate $x$ is
H transfer; $y$ and $z$ are independent promoting modes with separate masses and
reaction-path couplings. The analytic model or an external Hermitian diabatic PES
may supply $V_{11}$, $V_{22}$, and complex $V_{12}$.

The three-coordinate kinetic operator adds
$-(2m_z)^{-1}\partial_z^2$. Computational storage and FFT work grow with
$2N_xN_yN_z$, illustrating the exponential grid-growth problem for further direct
coordinates. Three dimensions therefore provide an explicit coupled-mode reference,
not a scalable replacement for reduced-dimensional models, tensor networks, or
multiconfiguration methods.

\code{potential_data.py} defines the external HDF5 schema, rejects missing or
incorrect units, validates finite monotone axes and array shapes, performs trilinear
interpolation, and converts angstrom/cm$^{-1}$ data to atomic units once. Thus
first-principles PES data can enter dynamics without manual unit handling. Electronic-
structure generation remains the responsibility of an external code; no dynamics
package can infer a first-principles PES from absent electronic-structure data.

Adiabatic energies alone do not uniquely determine a diabatic potential matrix:
electronic phases, state tracking, derivative couplings, and the chosen diabatization
fix the off-diagonal element and its sign or complex phase. Input surfaces must share
one smooth diabatic gauge across the full grid. Interpolation is numerical, not a
repair for discontinuous state labels or insufficient electronic-structure sampling.
These caveats follow the nonadiabatic-coupling analysis of Baer
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1016/S0370-1573(01)00052-7}{Phys. Rep. 358, 75 (2002)})
and modern first-principles diabatization discussion by Guan \emph{et al.}
(\href{https://doi.org/10.1039/D1CP03008F}{PCCP 23, 24962 (2021)}).

\section{Units and input boundary}

Namelist input uses chemistry-facing units and converts once to atomic units.
Output writers convert back to physical units.

Atomic units set $\hbar=e=m_e=4\pi\epsilon_0=1$, removing conversion factors from
propagators and bath equations. Conversion occurs once at the input boundary and
once at output. This avoids mixing a chemistry-scale time step with an atomic-unit
Hamiltonian, a failure that can remain numerically stable while being physically
wrong. Density conversion includes the Jacobian: if
$x_{\text{bohr}}=c x_{\text{\AA}}$, then
$\rho_{\text{\AA}^{-1}}=c\rho_{\text{bohr}^{-1}}$, preserving
$\int\rho(x)\,dx$.

\section{Parallel execution and reproducibility}

MPI distributes complete trajectories; OpenMP and threaded FFTW accelerate each
trajectory. Random streams depend on trajectory number rather than MPI rank.
Default output uses independent trajectory files. With Parallel HDF5 enabled,
ranks advance synchronized trajectory batches and write nonoverlapping
trajectory--frame--grid hyperslabs into one \code{*.ensemble.h5} file through
the HDF5 MPI-IO driver. A two-rank, three-trajectory regression checks layout,
physical units, finite values, and norms.

Versioned checkpoint files contain wavefunctions, intrinsic Fortran RNG state,
Langevin auxiliary variables, stochastic-potential state, time, step, and output
index. Each checkpoint is written to a temporary HDF5 file and installed by an
atomic rename. Deterministic FFTW plans permit bitwise comparison between
uninterrupted and restarted runs.

Trajectory-number-based random streams make stochastic results invariant to MPI
partitioning, while deterministic FFT planning stabilizes restart comparisons.
Bitwise restart identity is stronger than conservation alone: it verifies that hidden
Markov state---RNG position, OU variables, memory friction, output index, and both
wavefunctions---is restored. It does not imply bitwise portability across compilers,
FFTW builds, processor instruction sets, or floating-point contraction choices.

\section{Output and analysis}

HDF5 stores physical coordinates, times, wavefunctions, populations, position
expectations, and potential surfaces. Static potential surfaces are stored only
in the first frame; stochastic surfaces remain frame-resolved. HDF5 operations
fail loudly rather than silently leaving incomplete files. ASCII mode writes
snapshot densities, observable time series, and static or frame-resolved PES
files.

For pure states, stored coordinate densities are $|\psi_i(x,t)|^2$ and integrate to
diabatic populations. For density-matrix solvers they are diagonal coordinate-basis
elements, $\rho_i(x,x;t)$, and contain incoherent mixtures as well as coherent
wavepackets. Population traces alone discard spatial phase, intersurface coherence,
and energy redistribution; mechanism analysis should therefore retain density,
coherence, energy, and flux diagnostics when available.

\code{ShowDyn.py} reads trajectory files, displays HDF5 trees, plots snapshots,
animates densities and surfaces, and plots reactant/product probabilities.
\code{analyze_ensemble.py} combines rank/trajectory files or reads the parallel
single-file layout, checks common time
grids, writes mean and standard-error populations, performs bounded mono- and
biexponential fits, and can bootstrap trajectories for empirical rate intervals.

The bounded biexponential model used after detected onset $t_0$ is
\begin{equation}
 P_2(t)=P_2(t_0)+A_s\left[1-e^{-k_s(t-t_0)}\right]
                 +A_f\left[1-e^{-k_f(t-t_0)}\right],
 \quad 0\le A_s,A_f,\quad A_s+A_f\le1-P_2(t_0).
\end{equation}
Bounds prevent negative channel weights and populations above unity. AIC compares
mono- and biexponential descriptive models, while trajectory bootstrap probes
sampling uncertainty. Neither procedure identifies a microscopic channel: different
coherent, dissipative, or heterogeneous processes can generate nearly identical
population curves over a finite window.

\code{convergence_campaign.py} generates and executes time-step, grid, box,
absorber, and ensemble-size variants, then writes CSV and Markdown acceptance
tables. \code{mechanism_attribution.py} runs coupling-off, sub-barrier H,
sub-barrier D, and over-barrier controls. It combines classical Wigner
above-barrier fractions, prompt transfer, isotope suppression, background,
bounded rates, and SEM. A mechanism label is emitted only when all preregistered
tests pass; otherwise the result is explicitly inconclusive.

For the initial minimum-uncertainty Gaussian used here, the classical-contamination
estimate samples
\begin{equation}
 W(x,p)=\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\left[-\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{\sigma_0^2}
 -\sigma_0^2(p-p_0)^2\right]
\end{equation}
and counts samples satisfying $V_-(x)+p^2/(2m)\ge V_b$. This does not turn a Wigner
distribution into a classical ensemble; it provides a conservative diagnostic of how
much initial phase-space weight is already energetically above the selected adiabatic
barrier. Coupling-off control tests electronic-surface leakage, H/D comparison tests
mass sensitivity, and prompt high-energy transfer calibrates an over-barrier response.
No single diagnostic is unique, which is why attribution requires their conjunction.

\code{heom_convergence.py} runs hierarchy-depth, Matsubara-pole, and retained
system-basis variants. It gates population changes together with trace,
Hermiticity, and positivity errors.

Convergence has distinct numerical and statistical axes. Time step, grid spacing,
box size, basis size, hierarchy depth, and pole count control deterministic bias.
Trajectory count controls Monte Carlo uncertainty, which decreases only as
$N_{\rm traj}^{-1/2}$ for independent finite-variance samples. Passing one axis at a
time around an unconverged baseline can hide compensating errors; production work
should repeat coupled refinements and quote tolerances on observables used for the
final claim.

\section{Example two-timescale kinetics}

A 1.5 ps product-damped trajectory at 10 K produced a bounded
biexponential product-population fit with representative rates
\begin{align}
  k_{\rm fast} &\simeq 6.22\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{fs}^{-1},\\
  k_{\rm slow} &\simeq 2.47\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{fs}^{-1}.
\end{align}
The fit strongly improved AIC relative to a monoexponential model. Fast and slow
components are consistent with prompt/over-barrier and residual tunneling
channels, respectively, but this assignment is not unique from one stochastic
pure-state trajectory. Publication claims require ensemble statistics and
mechanism-control calculations.

A follow-up five-control campaign used four trajectories per control, 1.5~ps
duration, and two-SEM decision margins. Coupling-off background was zero and
the over-barrier gates passed. The tunneling gates failed: sub-barrier transfer
did not exceed estimated classical contamination by the required factor, and D
transfer was not suppressed relative to H. Therefore this parameter set supports
an over-barrier channel, while the slow biexponential component must remain an
empirical timescale rather than a demonstrated tunneling rate.

Rate separation is likewise not mechanism separation. When $k_f$ and $k_s$ approach
one another, or when one amplitude is small relative to ensemble noise, biexponential
parameters become strongly correlated and poorly identifiable. Stable mechanistic
interpretation requires persistence under fit-window changes, bootstrap resampling,
longer propagation, convergence refinement, and the independent barrier/coupling/
isotope controls above.

\section{Recommended production protocol}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Run \code{make test} for the selected compiler and library stack.
  \item Perform time-step, grid-spacing, box-size, and absorber convergence.
  \item Use several independent trajectories and aggregate with
        \code{analyze_ensemble.py}.
  \item Monitor total norm, absorbed norm, edge density, and high-frequency FFT content.
  \item Compare no-damping, reactant-only, product-only, and both-state damping.
  \item Establish mechanism with barrier-energy and coupling controls before
        labeling fitted rates as over-barrier or tunneling.
  \item Archive input, executable revision, dependency versions, seeds, raw data,
        and analysis outputs.
\end{enumerate}
